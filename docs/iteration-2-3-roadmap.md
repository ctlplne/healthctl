# healthctl · combined iteration 2 and 3

Proposed scope for one local release, prepared 21 November 2026. The original [PRD, sections 1–16](../PRD.md) remains the baseline. For delivered features and open checks, see the [combined verification report](combined-verification.md) and [model evaluation](../evaluation/README.md).

## Product and first users

An English-speaking adult can prepare for a visit, inspect exactly how their words were understood, explore possible explanations and relevant medical evidence, take a concise brief to a clinician, and return after the visit. The intended first users are adults preparing for outpatient visits. A clinician receiving the brief is a second audience for that artifact. A self-hosting practice or integrator is a potential distribution partner. The first pilot can use patient-controlled installations and manually shared briefs without pooling patient records.

V1 already has adaptive intake, exact-quote correction, warnings, revisioned provisional estimates, three model adapters and private JSON and print exports. Eight browser journeys passed. The 19 synthetic cases ran twice with a deterministic model. Only one fixture case had an authored differential. No real model was exercised in the recorded run. The clinical warning policy still needs professional review. These gaps set the order of work.

## The acceptance journey

A returning patient starts a new concern, reviews which medications and earlier events still apply, and pastes part of a visit note. The application proposes dated facts alongside the original passages. The patient corrects “I had a blood clot” to “my father had a blood clot.” The earlier note and prior encounter stay intact. The current reviewed history distinguishes family history from personal history. An estimate generated before that correction becomes visibly outdated.

The new interview asks only questions that could affect urgency or the assessment. An urgent warning interrupts it. Once the patient reviews sufficient history, they can deliberately open the provisional differential with the warning still visible. They can inspect the patient statement supporting a fact and, separately, the medical passage supporting an explanation. If evidence or history is insufficient, the application says which is missing.

The patient exports a one-page brief containing the correction, outstanding questions and warning. A clinician can use it without an account. At home the patient records the visit outcome with its date and source. The original assessment remains available as it appeared at the time. This whole journey also works with an interrupted model call, refresh, retry and restored backup.

## Start an interview from a medical report

**Core feature for the combined release.** Offer “Start with a report” alongside “Describe a concern.” Patients can also add a report midway through an interview. Ask what they want to understand, whether the report belongs to them, and what prompted the test. Keep a routine screening visit possible even when the patient has no symptoms.

1. **Read the report locally.** Accept PDF, DOCX, plain-text files and pasted text. Use local optical character recognition (OCR) for scanned PDFs and photos of printed pages. Cover blood and urine test panels, written imaging and pathology reports, discharge summaries and visit notes. For an unfamiliar layout, offer passage selection or manual transcription with a source reference. The image feature reads writing on a report page. Interpretation of the underlying X-ray, slide or scan is outside this release.
2. **Let the patient check the extraction.** Show each selected finding beside its original line, table row or page region. For labs, preserve the printed test name, result (including qualifiers such as “<” or “trace”), units, the laboratory's reference range and flag, and the specimen collection date when available. Track the issue date separately. Use the report's own labels, including unfamiliar panel abbreviations. Highlight uncertain digits, row alignment, dates and patient identity for review. Leave missing information unknown. Keep a correction or disagreement alongside the original. Confirming a transcription records what the document says.
3. **Use the report to choose the next question.** Combine the patient's concern, history already given and reviewed report entries. Ask about gaps that could change urgency or assessment. For example, if the patient already described symptoms and the urine report gives a collection date, ask whether the symptoms started before or after that sample. If the patient already supplied that timing, skip the question. Let “Why this question?” open the specific report row and explain the missing context. If a report and the patient's recollection disagree, ask about the discrepancy. A document flag or lab result alone must never create a patient-reported symptom or a confirmed diagnosis.
4. **Carry the source through the visit.** Label findings from the report, the patient's own account and model interpretations separately in the differential and the exported brief. A corrected or withdrawn entry invalidates any dependent current assessment and triggers review of follow-ups. Route urgent information through the reviewed warning policy, including at upload, without making the patient finish transcription first. Preserve the existing warning and differential-viewing behavior.
5. **Keep the patient in control of disclosure.** Keep original files and OCR on the installation, behind encounter authorization and covered by encryption, backup and deletion. Before a remote model call, preview which selected extracts will be sent. Exclude unselected pages and identifying headers by default. Confirm any change of provider or endpoint before sending existing encounter content.

## Build order inside one release

1. **Qualify the starting point.** Repeat the v1 journey on the rebuilt app against one real remote model with synthetic cases. Recruit clinical reviewers, agree the first pathways, and freeze the benchmark before tuning. Outputs are a baseline report and explicit coverage limits.
2. **Finish the report-first and returning-patient journeys.** Deliver upload and local extraction, source review, report-guided questions, timeline, reconciliation, warning handling, visual revision comparison, brief and follow-up. Test patient comprehension and clinician reading time using fictional scenarios.
3. **Add evidence and integration to that journey.** Start with a small reviewed collection. Verify citations and abstention, introduce scoped report access and test one local consumer. Keep medical content, patient statements and model-generated hypotheses distinct through export.
4. **Exercise the release as an operator and a clinic would.** Rebuild containers, upgrade a v1 database, restore a backup, rotate a key, simulate provider and evidence outages, and repeat desktop, mobile, accessibility and clinical-content evaluations. Publish the results into the local release package.

For planning, start with four candidate adult pathways covering respiratory symptoms, headache, abdominal symptoms and dizziness. Include urgent look-alikes, recent medication changes and histories that support no useful estimate in each review. Ask the clinical lead to approve coverage and exclusions before authoring patient-facing guidance. A pathway counts as covered only when its questions, warnings, differential behavior, references and handoff have all been exercised together.

## How to judge the release

| Question | Test and decision |
|---|---|
| Can we trust the record? | Retain all v1 software checks. Across required cases and export types, any observed invented patient finding, stale assessment presented as current, or lost urgent warning blocks the candidate until resolved and rerun. List the case, failed step, model and affected output individually. |
| Does the report actually change the interview? | Use fictional digital and scanned reports, including a multi-column panel, a blurred decimal, a missing unit, conflicting dates and a wrong-patient document. Compare the same patient story with and without a relevant report and with an unrelated report. Check which questions were asked or skipped and their specific sources. Correct or withdraw a row mid-interview and verify the resulting questions, assessment revision, warning and exported attribution. Check the remote request contains only approved extracts and existing authorized history. Report individual extraction, attribution and questioning failures. |
| Is the interview worth the patient's effort? | Pair opening-only and adaptive interviews on the same model. Report important facts elicited, repeated questions, median and high-end completion time, abandonment and appropriate abstention. Compare against v1 and against a short static form. |
| Are the clinical capabilities fit for the intended scope? | Have independent clinicians adjudicate a held-out set spanning common, urgent, ambiguous, out-of-scope and insufficient histories. Set thresholds for missed urgency, excessive urgency, appropriate abstention, materially misleading explanations and unsupported citations before scoring. Report case counts, uncertainty, reviewer disagreements, and results by complaint and relevant group. Sweep multiple runs per included model. |
| Does the handoff improve the visit? | In a blinded comparison, clinicians read the v1 report and new brief for matched fictional cases. Measure time to recover essential facts, consequential omissions and questions they still need to repeat. Have patients explain the warning and the provisional nature of the result in their own words. |
| Is it affordable in practice? | Track requests, input/output tokens, retries, median and high-end latency, cost per completed encounter and cost per clinician-acceptable brief. Include failed calls, infrastructure and content maintenance. Compare models on the same cases at an agreed quality threshold. Keep measurements local. |
| Is it ready to distribute? | Demonstrate fresh install, upgrade, restore, deletion, consent on destination changes, protected imports/exports and rollback. For real-world use, resolve intended use, jurisdiction-specific regulatory and privacy review, content rights, reviewer ownership and incident response. The FDA explicitly addresses patient-facing functions. [FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software) |

## Commercial and scope decisions

Reduce cost in ways an operator can verify. Build the literature index once per content version, retrieve passages locally, render briefs from confirmed structured facts, and reuse an existing assessment only for its unchanged input and configuration. Cache shareable literature data separately from patient material. Run candidate models on the same held-out cases before recommending a lower-cost configuration. Show the operator the cost of retries and abandoned interviews as well as successful ones. If a cheaper setup misses more warnings or produces worse briefs, that difference belongs next to its price.

Ship the interview engine, corrections, warnings, visit brief, evidence retrieval, BYOM adapters, APIs and synthetic evaluation tools in the source-available distribution. Each software version uses BUSL-1.1 and converts to MPL-2.0 four years after its first public distribution. Corpus licenses are tracked separately. The distribution notice records the licensor, personal and internal use grant, and separate permission for third-party hosting or commercial service embedding. Paid deployment help, support, maintained content or qualification services are potential offerings. Price them only after measuring delivery costs and buyer demand. [Existing release decisions](release.md)

My scope recommendation is a personal deployment plus one read-only integration in this combined release. A shared clinic inbox is a separate buying decision. Build patient identity separation, roles, invitations, revocation, patient-to-clinician assignment and access auditing before using a shared installation with multiple patients. Start additional languages with human-reviewed interview and warning content, region-appropriate navigation and separate qualification. Scheduling, EHR write-back, prescribing, pediatric or pregnancy-specific estimates, medical image interpretation and ambient recording each need their own intended use, partner or validation. A partner request and evidence of a workflow benefit would justify moving one of these forward.
